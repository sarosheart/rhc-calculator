    About this simulator — how the Sentinel works, the models, and references

    Two filters for the vessels to the brain

    During a TAVR, crossing and dilating the calcified valve, placing the new one and moving large catheters around the arch can knock debris loose toward the brain. The Sentinel cerebral embolic protection device puts two cone-shaped filters in the way: a proximal filter in the innominate (brachiocephalic) artery, which protects the right carotid and right vertebral arteries, and a distal filter in the left common carotid. Both open with their mouths facing the aorta, so blood bound for the head passes through them. The left vertebral artery, which arises from the left subclavian, is not covered. The device is a 6F catheter placed over a 0.014-inch wire from the right radial artery, at about LAO 30, with the ACT kept above 250 seconds until it is out. This page shows the handle and the arch side by side, so each move of the hands can be matched with what the filters and the articulating sheath do in the vessels at that moment.

    Deployment, step by step

    • Position: the catheter is advanced with the wire about 10 cm ahead until the sheathed proximal filter sits in the innominate and the articulating sheath points down the ascending aorta.
    • Slider 1: pulled back, it uncovers the proximal filter in a straight stretch 1.5 to 2 cm past the innominate's origin.
    • Rear handle, a little: pulling it back brings the articulating sheath up out of the ascending aorta.
    • Knob 2: turning it curls the articulating sheath.
    • Rear handle turn: turning the rear handle points the curl at the left carotid; the wire is pushed in, no more than 5 cm.
    • Rear handle, fully: the sheath's U comes to rest on the carina between the innominate and the left carotid, its tip just inside the left carotid.
    • Slider 3: advanced, it pushes the distal filter out of the sheath into the left carotid; the wire is pulled back to about 1 cm past the tip and the rear lock is closed.

    During the TAVR, and retrieval

    With both filters open, the TAVR goes ahead; the pigtail lies across the arch in the non-coronary cusp and the delivery catheter runs past the filter mouths. Retrieval reverses deployment: Slider 3 back to draw the distal filter into the articulating sheath, the rear handle forward to close the gap, Knob 2 back to straighten the sheath while the wire is pushed ahead, Slider 1 forward to cover the proximal filter, then the whole device out under fluoroscopy, holding Slider 1, with the wire about 10 cm ahead of the tip.

    What the filters catch

    When the filters are examined under the microscope, most hold some material, and most particles are 0.15 to 0.5 mm across. What it is varies from patient to patient: in one series of 161 patients (ALSTER), thrombus was found in 91%, arterial wall tissue in 68%, valve tissue in 53%, calcium in 46% and catheter material in 30%. In randomized trials the device has not significantly lowered the rate of stroke after TAVR (SENTINEL, PROTECTED TAVR and BHF PROTECT-TAVI), which is why its use is selective.

    About the models

    The arch is built from published measurements of a TAVR-age, type I arch, not from any patient's scan: the root and its three sinuses, the ascending aorta, the arch, the innominate with the right subclavian, right vertebral and right carotid, the left carotid, and the left subclavian with the left vertebral. The front of every vessel is cut away so the lumen shows. The device's published numbers are used where they exist (6F, a 15 mm proximal and 10 mm distal filter, 4.0 and 4.5 cm long, a 4.0 cm articulating sheath). No dimensions are published for the handle, so its parts and their travel are estimates from the usage guide's photographs, and how far the rear handle turns is drawn as half a turn. Debris is drawn five times life size so it shows on a phone. The new TAVR valve itself is not drawn. Every position of every part was checked against the vessel walls before it was drawn.
