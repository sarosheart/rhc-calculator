    About this simulator — how each closure works, the models, and references

    Why closure is hard to picture

    Every step of a femoral closure happens under the skin. The hands feel a lever lift, a plunger travel, a strand tighten; the eyes see a handle and a drip of blood. This page shows the two side by side: the device as you hold it, and a cutaway of the right groin with the artery or vein cut lengthwise, so each move of the hands can be matched with what the device does in the vessel wall at that moment. Every step follows the manufacturer's instructions for use, and each device offers the common mistakes so you can watch what goes wrong mechanically, then undo them.

    Perclose ProGlide: one stitch through the front wall

    The device goes in over a wire until blood comes back from its marker lumen. Lifting the lever opens a foot inside the artery, which is pulled up against the inside of the front wall. Pushing the plunger drives two needles from outside the vessel through the wall, one on each side of the hole, into cuffs at the ends of the foot. Pulling the plunger out brings back the anterior needle with a white link and the suture: one loop goes down through one bite, under the hole and up through the other. A knot is already tied on the device. Of the two strands that come out, the long blue one is the rail: pulled taut against a knot pusher, it slides the knot down to the artery. The short strand with the white tip locks the knot. It stays slack until the knot is down, and it is pulled only after the wire is out.

    Pre-close for large-bore access

    Above 8F in an artery, or 14F in a vein, two ProGlides are placed before the large sheath goes in. Each is turned about 30° on its own axis, one each way, so the two stitches cross in an X over the hole. Their strands are clamped with shodded hemostats and parked. At the end of the case the knots are advanced without locking, the wire comes out once the bleeding is controlled, and the knots are locked in the order the stitches were placed.

    Angio-Seal VIP: anchor, collagen and suture

    The insertion sheath and locator are advanced until blood drips from the drip hole, then set at the depth where it just starts. The device releases a small absorbable anchor inside the artery; pulled back, it lies flat against the inside of the front wall. Withdrawing the device leaves a collagen sponge in the tract, and the tamper tube presses it onto the outside of the hole while the suture is held taut. The hole is sandwiched between anchor and collagen, and all three parts are absorbed within about 60 to 90 days. The black marker on the suture is not absorbable and is cut off below the skin.

    The figure-of-eight venous stitch

    After a large venous sheath, two bites are taken through the subcutaneous tissue, medial to lateral, about 1 to 1.5 cm deep and superficial to the vessels: one just caudal and one just cranial to where the sheath enters the skin, with the suture crossing over the sheath between them. Tied down as the sheath comes out, the stitch gathers the skin and fat over the venotomy into a tamponade. The vein wall itself is not stitched, and the stitch is removed after a few hours.

    About the models

    The ProGlide's working end is built to Abbott's published numbers: an 8F guide, needles that each take about 2 mm of wall beside the hole, 8.5 mm between the outer edges of the two bites, and the device at 45° to the skin. No dimensions are published for the ProGlide handle, the Suture Trimmer or any Angio-Seal part, so those are estimates from product photographs; the Angio-Seal anchor is drawn about 10 mm long and the compacted collagen about 6 by 4 mm. The femoral artery is drawn 7 mm across with a 1 mm wall, 30 mm under the skin. Where the instructions do not say exactly how a part moves, such as how the pre-tied knot cinches or how the anchor sits as it catches on the sheath, the page shows the reading that fits the instructions and the manufacturers' drawings.
