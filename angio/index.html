About this simulator — angiographic views, how the model is built, and credits

What the C-arm angles do

A coronary angiogram is a shadow. The X-ray tube sits under the table and the detector above the patient, and the picture on the monitor is the coronary tree, filled with contrast, flattened onto the detector. Because the arteries run over a curved heart, one projection never shows every segment well. The operator moves the C-arm in two ways. Rotation swings the detector toward the patient's left (left anterior oblique, LAO) or right (right anterior oblique, RAO). Angulation tilts it toward the head (cranial) or the feet (caudal). Each standard view is a pair of these numbers, and this page shows what each pair does to the picture.

Foreshortening and overlap

Two things make a projection good or poor for a given segment. A segment running toward or away from the detector looks shorter than it is, which is foreshortening; a lesion inside a foreshortened segment can look shorter or milder than it is. And a segment hidden behind another vessel, or behind its own branches, cannot be judged at all, which is overlap. The standard views exist because each one lays a particular part of the tree out flat and clear of its neighbours:

  • RAO caudal opens the left main bifurcation, the proximal circumflex and the obtuse marginals.
  • RAO and AP cranial stretch the mid and distal LAD and separate the diagonals from it.
  • LAO cranial shows the LAD and its diagonals from the left, along the interventricular groove.
  • LAO caudal, the spider view, looks up at the left main and the origins of the LAD and circumflex.
  • For the right coronary artery, LAO shows the whole C-shaped course in the right atrioventricular groove, RAO lays out the mid vessel, and cranial angulation opens the crux, where the posterior descending and posterolateral branches arise.

Real anatomy varies, so these are starting points. In the lab the operator adjusts by a few degrees until the segment of interest is long, straight and alone.

How to use it

Drag the rotation scale or the angulation scale, tap a point on either to jump there, type an angle such as LAO 45 or CAU 30, or use the arrow keys (hold Shift for five-degree steps). The view cards set both angles at once, and a right-coronary card switches the angiogram to the right system. The 3D heart turns with your mouse or finger, and the Detector view button lines it up with the angiogram. Every view has its own link, so the address bar can be shared.

Tap a vessel on the angiogram, or a row in the Segments list, to select its segment: it is highlighted in the angiogram and the 3D heart, and the angle pad shades every C-arm angle by how well that segment is seen there. Labels and Colour mark every segment by its SYNTAX number and its artery.

Dominance, ramus and grafts

Dominance is named for the artery that crosses the crux to give the posterior descending artery (PDA) and the posterolateral branches. When the right coronary gives the PDA and the circumflex gives posterolateral branches, the heart is called co-dominant (balanced). Switch between right-, left- and co-dominant hearts to see how the crux changes: in a left-dominant heart the circumflex wraps round to give the PDA and the right coronary is small. Add a ramus intermedius, a third branch of the left main, to see where it falls between the LAD and the circumflex. Add bypass grafts (a LIMA to the LAD, and vein grafts to an obtuse marginal and to the PDA) to see where each lies at a given angle; each graft is listed with the artery it was grafted to, and the sternal wires and the clips on the LIMA appear as metal.

Real angiograms and the room

Beside the simulated angiogram, the 3D heart can give its place to a real angiogram or to the room. Real angiogram shows the frame nearest the current angle from 160 key frames of the CARDIAG dataset, recorded in real cath labs, with the angle it was taken at and how far that is from yours. Farther and Nearer (or a sideways swipe) step through the next nearest frames, Go to this angle moves the C-arm to the frame, and Outlines shows each segment as the dataset's experts labelled it; tap an outline to select that segment everywhere (when this heart shows it). Each frame is a real patient, not the model's heart, so lengths and branches differ from the model and from frame to frame.

Room shows the patient on the table with the C-arm around them and the beam through the heart. The X-ray tube is opposite the detector: under the table in the usual views. The view is named for where the detector sits, so in LAO it is over the patient's left side and in a cranial view it is tilted toward the head. Rotation turns the C-arm about the patient's long axis and angulation tilts it toward the head or the feet. Drag the detector to move the C-arm: seen from the operator's side the drag tilts it, seen from the feet it rotates it, and Both lets it do either.

Reading the numbers

For each segment the page computes two numbers at the exact angle on screen. Foreshortened is how much shorter the segment looks than it really is: 0% means it lies flat to the detector, and 50% means it looks half its length because it runs partly along the X-ray beam. Overlapped is how much of the segment's shadow is crossed by other branches of the same coronary artery (left or right), in front of it or behind it. A segment at 25% or more on either number is listed as foreshortened or overlapped rather than seen clearly. Where a published standard view promises a segment and this model disagrees at that angle, the page says so: the published views describe typical anatomy, and one average heart will not match every one of them.

How the model is built

Everything on this page comes from open data. The heart, great vessels and chest come from BodyParts3D, an anatomical atlas made from a full-body scan. The course of the coronary arteries comes from the ImageCAS-X dataset: 773 coronary CT angiograms were aligned to each other and averaged by dominance (706 right-dominant scans, 39 left-dominant and 28 co-dominant, one tree for each), then laid onto the atlas heart, so no single patient's arteries are shown. Vessel calibres follow the measurements of normal human coronary arteries published by Dodge and colleagues in 1992. The X-ray picture is computed, not drawn: for each pixel the page adds up how much contrast, bone and soft tissue the beam crosses and dims the image accordingly, so crossing vessels look darker, as they do on a real cine frame. Its appearance was checked against real angiogram frames from the CARDIAG dataset. The bypass grafts are built from measurements of 16 post-operative models in the Vascular Model Repository (where vein grafts leave the aorta, their angles, lengths and calibres); no model's geometry is used. The LIMA keeps its origin from the subclavian artery and runs down beside the sternum before crossing to the LAD, as a pedicled graft does.

Limits

This is a teaching model: one atlas heart carrying an averaged coronary tree for each dominance, still and without motion. It does not show disease, collateral flow, catheter position or the variation between patients, and it is not a substitute for supervised training in the cath lab. The room is a sketch: the patient and the C-arm are simple shapes, and collisions with the table or the patient are not modelled.
